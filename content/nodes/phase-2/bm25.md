# What is BM25?

BM25 is a formula for ranking documents by keyword match, and one of the
most successful ever made. It scores a document on which query words it
contains, how rare those words are in the collection, and how long the
document is. It needs no model and no training, and it's the keyword half
of [[hybrid-search|hybrid search]].

## A query where counting words goes wrong

Say you have 1,000 technical documents and someone searches for "database
connection pooling". Two candidates:

- **Document A** is a long article about databases in general, three times
  the average length. It says "database" 50 times, "connection" 3 times and
  "pooling" once.
- **Document B** is a guide to connection pooling, of average length. It
  says "database" 5 times, "connection" 4 times and "pooling" 12 times.

Count matching words and A wins, 54 to 21. That's wrong: B is the page
the person wants. BM25 gets it right with three ideas.

## Rare words count more

"database" appears in 400 of the 1,000 documents, so it tells you little.
"pooling" appears in only 10, so a document with it is much more likely to
be about the question. BM25 weights each query word by how many documents
contain it. This is the **inverse document frequency** (IDF):

$$
\text{IDF}(t) = \ln \frac{N - n_t + 0.5}{n_t + 0.5}
$$

Here $$N$$ is the number of documents and $$n_t$$ is how many contain the
word $$t$$. In our collection, "database" weighs 0.41, "connection" (in 100
documents) weighs 2.19, and "pooling" weighs 4.55. One mention of
"pooling" is worth about eleven of "database".

## Repeats stop helping

The first time a word shows up in a document is strong evidence. The
fiftieth adds almost nothing. So BM25 puts the count $$tf$$ (term
frequency) through a curve that rises fast and then flattens toward a
ceiling:

$$
\frac{tf}{k_1 + tf}
$$

$$k_1$$ is a knob, commonly 1.2. Then one mention scores 0.45 of the
ceiling, five score 0.81, and fifty score 0.98: about twice one. A smaller
$$k_1$$ flattens the curve sooner.

This is called **saturation**, and it's the main thing that sets BM25
apart from older tf-idf scoring, which lets the count keep adding up.

## Long documents get a discount

A long document matches more query words by chance. So BM25 compares each
document's length with the collection's average, and raises $$k_1$$ for
long documents and lowers it for short ones. A second knob, $$b$$, sets how
much: at 1 length counts fully, at 0 it's off, and 0.75 is common. The
middle ground is on purpose. Some long documents are just wordy and
deserve the discount. Others are long because they cover more ground, and
their extra matches are real. With $$b = 0.75$$, one mention in a document
twice the average length scores 0.32 instead of 0.45.

## Putting it together

A document's score adds up, over the query words, each word's weight times
its saturated, length-adjusted count ($$|D|$$ is the document's length):

$$
\text{score}(D, Q) = \sum_{t \in Q} \text{IDF}(t) \cdot \frac{tf_{t,D}}{k_1 \left( (1 - b) + b \cdot \frac{|D|}{\text{avgdl}} \right) + tf_{t,D}}
$$

For our two documents, with $$k_1 = 1.2$$ and $$b = 0.75$$:

| Word | IDF | A: count | A: score | B: count | B: score |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| database | 0.41 | 50 | 0.38 | 5 | 0.33 |
| connection | 2.19 | 3 | 1.10 | 4 | 1.69 |
| pooling | 4.55 | 1 | 1.14 | 12 | 4.13 |
| **Total** | | 54 | **2.62** | 21 | **6.15** |

The guide wins by more than two to one.

Many versions multiply the fraction by $$(k_1 + 1)$$. That scales every
score equally, so the ranking doesn't change.

## Where it gets tricky

**It only matches words.** BM25 treats a document as a bag of words: which
ones appear and how often, not what they mean. A search for "car" won't
find a page that only says "automobile". Finding meaning is what
[[semantic-search]] does. The flip side is that BM25 is exact: an error
code, a product ID or a person's name matches only itself.

**Common words can go negative.** In the formula above, a word in more
than half the documents gets a weight below zero (−2.19 for a word in 900
of our 1,000). Published versions of BM25 differ in details like this, so
check what your engine does.

**The knobs have no theory behind them.** Experiments found $$k_1$$ from
1.2 to 2 and $$b$$ from 0.5 to 0.8 work in many cases, but the best values
depend on your documents and queries.

**Postgres's built-in text ranking isn't BM25.** `ts_rank` has no IDF, no
saturation and no normalization by average document length, so the long,
repetitive document A would beat the guide. Its `@@` match also requires
every query word, so a great page missing one word is dropped before
ranking starts. For real BM25 inside Postgres you need an extension such as
pg_textsearch (Tiger Data, first released 2025-10) or ParadeDB's pg_search.

## What this means when you build

- BM25 is cheap: no model, no GPU, no embedding calls. Run it as your
  baseline before anything fancier.
- Keep the common defaults ($$k_1 = 1.2$$, $$b = 0.75$$) unless you have
  labeled queries to tune on.
- Pair it with vector search to catch paraphrases: [[hybrid-search]].
- On Postgres, check whether your "full-text search" is BM25 or `ts_rank`.
