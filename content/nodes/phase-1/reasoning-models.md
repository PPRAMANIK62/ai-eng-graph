# What are reasoning models?

A reasoning model writes out a private chain of reasoning, sometimes thousands
of tokens long, before it gives you the answer. It was trained to do this, and
trained to make that reasoning useful. On math, code and multi-step problems
it's much more accurate than a regular model. You pay for it in time and
money, because every one of those thinking tokens is generated and billed,
even the ones you never see.

## One request, with and without thinking

Ask a model: "What is the greatest common divisor of 1071 and 462?"

A regular model starts writing the answer right away. A reasoning model first
produces a block of thinking. With Claude, you can ask to see a summary of it,
which for this question looks something like:

> Use Euclidean algorithm. 1071 = 2*462 + 147. 462 = 3*147 + 21. 147 = 7*21
> + 0. GCD = 21.

Then comes the answer you actually show your user: the GCD is 21.

The thinking is scratch work. The model restates the problem, tries an
approach, checks intermediate results, and drops paths that don't work, all
before committing to an answer. A regular model has to get it right in one
go. It can't check or change course partway without writing that into the
answer itself.

## How is this different from chain-of-thought prompting?

You've seen the idea before. With [[chain-of-thought]] prompting, you ask a
regular model to "think step by step" and it writes its reasoning into the
answer. That helps on some tasks, but nothing in the model's training
specifically rewarded reasoning that ends in the right answer.

A reasoning model is trained, during [[post-training]], specifically to
produce reasoning that leads to correct answers. The reasoning also comes
back separately from the answer, as its own block, rather than mixed into the
text you show users.

## How they're trained: reward the right answer, let the reasoning find its way

The key ingredient is reinforcement learning on problems with checkable
answers. The loop is simple:

1. Give the model a batch of problems, such as math questions with known
   answers or coding tasks with unit tests.
2. Let it write several attempts at each, with reasoning.
3. Check each final answer automatically. Correct gets a reward, wrong gets
   nothing.
4. Nudge the model toward the attempts that were correct. Repeat, revisiting
   the same problems many times.

Nothing in this loop grades the reasoning itself. Only the final answer is
checked. The model is free to reason however it likes, and it gets pushed
toward whatever reasoning tends to end in right answers.

DeepSeek's R1 paper (2025) is the most detailed public account. They started
from a base model with no reasoning examples at all, used only this kind of
reward, and asked for reasoning inside think tags followed by an answer. Over
training:

- Accuracy on AIME 2024, a hard math competition, rose from 15.6% to 77.9%.
- The model's answers got steadily longer. Nobody told it to think more; it
  learned that longer reasoning paid off, growing to hundreds or thousands of
  tokens.
- It started re-checking its own work. At one point the word "wait" suddenly
  became common in its reasoning, as it caught and revisited its own steps.
  The authors called it an "aha moment".

They deliberately didn't use a learned reward model to score the reasoning
tasks, because they'd seen models learn to game learned reward models during
large-scale training. A
simple correctness check is hard to game.

The first successful models trained this way were OpenAI's o1 and DeepSeek's
R1, and most labs followed during 2025.

## Reasoning can be copied into smaller models

Once you have a strong reasoning model, you can use its outputs to train
smaller ones. DeepSeek fine-tuned small open models on 800,000 examples
generated by R1, with no reinforcement learning at all. The smallest, 1.5
billion parameters, scored 28.9% on AIME 2024, against 9.3% for GPT-4o and
16.0% for Claude 3.5 Sonnet (the 2024 versions compared in the paper).
This is called distillation.

## Thinking longer is spending compute at answer time

A reasoning model spends more compute on each answer: more tokens, so more
passes through the network, before it commits. This is often called
test-time or inference-time compute.

That trade pays off on some questions and not others. A 2024 study found that
on easy and medium questions, a small model allowed to think more could beat
a model 14 times its size. On the hardest questions, extra thinking barely
helped, and the bigger model won. For those, a more capable model matters
more than more thinking.

The models also adjust on their own. R1 uses fewer tokens on simple tasks and
more on complex ones, though it still sometimes overthinks easy questions.

## What it costs you (as of 2026-09)

Both big providers work the same way on the points that matter for your bill:

| | OpenAI | Anthropic (Claude) |
|---|---|---|
| Thinking billed as | Output tokens | Output tokens |
| Counts toward output limit | Yes, `max_output_tokens` | Yes, `max_tokens` |
| Can you see the raw reasoning? | No, only an optional summary | No, only an optional summary |
| Control | `reasoning.effort`, from `none` to `max` on some models | `effort`, plus thinking on or off on some models |
| Default | Medium effort on GPT-5.5 and GPT-5.6 | Thinking on by default on Opus 5.5 and Sonnet 5 |

A few details that bite:

- **You pay for thinking you don't see.** On Claude, a summary is written by a
  separate model, and you're billed for the full thinking, not the summary.
  Hiding the thinking entirely makes responses start sooner but costs the
  same.
- **Thinking can eat your whole output budget.** Reasoning can run from a few
  hundred to tens of thousands of tokens. On OpenAI's API, if it hits the
  output limit you can pay for input and reasoning and get no visible answer.
  OpenAI suggests reserving at least 25,000 tokens when you start out.
- **Thinking takes context space.** The current turn's thinking sits in the
  context window like any other output.
- **Effort is a hint, the token limit is a wall.** Lower effort makes the
  model think less and respond faster and cheaper, but only the output limit
  is a hard cap.
- **Some models won't switch it off.** As of 2026-09, Claude Opus 5.5 rejects
  requests that disable thinking. Sonnet 5 allows it.

## When to use one

OpenAI's framing is a useful rule of thumb: reasoning models are the
planners, regular models the workhorses. Regular models are faster and
cheaper and handle well-defined tasks well. Reasoning models are better at
complex, ambiguous, multi-step problems and at decisions where accuracy
matters more than speed. Many workflows use both: a reasoning model to plan or
decide, regular models to carry out the steps.

They also want different prompts:

- **Don't ask them to think step by step.** They already do; asking is
  unnecessary.
- **Try zero-shot first.** They often don't need examples. DeepSeek found
  few-shot examples consistently made R1 worse, and recommends describing
  the problem directly.
- **Give the goal and constraints, not every step.** Say what a good result
  looks like and let the model work out how to get there.

## Where it gets tricky

**You can't read the real reasoning.** Both providers show a summary at most.
Even if you could read all of it, the written reasoning doesn't always
reflect what drove the answer, as covered in [[chain-of-thought]].

**The gains are biggest where answers can be checked.** Training rewards
correct answers on math, code and logic. For open-ended writing, there's no
reliable automatic check, which makes this kind of training hard to apply.

**More thinking isn't always better.** Past a point, extra thinking adds cost
and delay without better answers, and on the hardest questions it may not
help at all. Only your own evals can tell you where that point is for your
task.

**The details change fast.** Model names, effort levels, defaults and which
models allow thinking to be turned off differ from one model version to the
next. Claude's older fixed thinking budget, for example, is rejected by Claude
4.7 and later. Check the docs for the exact model you use.

## What this means when you build

- **Match the model to the task.** Use a reasoning model where accuracy on
  hard, multi-step work matters. Use a regular model, or low effort, for
  simple lookups, classification and chat where speed matters.
- **Start with low or medium effort and raise it only if evals improve.**
- **Budget output tokens generously** and handle the case where a response
  comes back cut off with no answer.
- **Watch reasoning tokens in the usage data.** That's where the cost hides.
- **Prompt plainly.** Clear goal, constraints and output format. No "think
  step by step", and try without examples first.
